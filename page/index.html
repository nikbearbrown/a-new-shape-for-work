A New Shape for Work

How AI is Affecting Jobs. Where a job's work sits across six phases, estimated from O*NET task ratings, set beside what job postings ask for and where AI use already shows up. Every effort number here is a model, labelled as one.

Executive summary

  • It works end to end on real data. O*NET task-based effort profiles exist for all 911 occupations O*NET rates (18,420 rated tasks). The posting layer covers every occupation O*NET names a title for: 35% of 1.15 million US LinkedIn postings were assigned to an occupation by title, giving 429 occupations with 50 or more postings. All nine charts in the brief are drawn.
  • The calibration case lands in range, loosely. Software developers come out at 16% Produce (main model) and 22% under the occurrence model, inside the 15–30% hands-on coding range quoted in the brief. The flat and ordinal models agree. The task list itself is the weak part: 7 of its 17 tasks are conferring, supervising or reporting.
  • The layers disagree, and that is the interesting part. Loading posting results…
  • No experience creep in the dated sample, but "entry level" is not entry level. Across 34,960 data-analyst postings (Nov 2022 to Apr 2025) the median stated minimum stayed at 3 years and 88–91% asked for 2 or more. In the January 2024 LinkedIn snapshot, 75% of entry-titled software developer postings and 75% of entry-titled accountant postings that state a minimum ask for 2+ years.
  • The Skills Lexicon is missing common posting terms. Only 8–33% of skill mentions matched an entry. Git, Kubernetes, Jenkins, nursing and patient care have no entry, and "Linux" resolves to "Linux systems administration".
  • The AI overlay is built. Using the Anthropic Economic Index's task coverage, 35% of software developers' estimated effort sits in tasks with observed Claude use, against 6% for registered nurses and 0% for electricians. 435 of 911 occupations have no covered task.
  • Nothing here is reviewed. The 2,087 work-activity phase tags and the skill-phrase tags are drafts written today; posting skills are the dataset's own model-extracted lists, not employer text. Treat every chart as a picture of the method, not a finding about the labor market.

The three models differ only in how O*NET's seven frequency categories become weight. Ordinal uses the category number (1 = yearly or less, 7 = hourly or more), so a daily task counts about twice a monthly one. Occurrence converts categories to times per year (1, 4, 24, 100, 250, 750, 2000), so hourly micro-tasks dominate. Flat ignores frequency. All three multiply by importance and by the share of incumbents who say the task is part of the job. None of them is time.

Where this prototype breaks

Analysis is contested

O*NET's "Analyzing Data or Information" is tagged Understand. For data scientists that sends half the job to Understand and none to Check, because analysis is their product. One tag moves a whole family.

Coarse task lists

Data scientists have 16 rated tasks and information security analysts 11. With so few tasks, a single tag decides a fifth of the profile.

Work-activity ratings don't separate jobs

A cross-check built from O*NET's 41 work-activity importance ratings gave almost the same profile for every occupation (about 13 / 17 / 29 / 11 / 9 / 20). Only the task layer distinguishes jobs, so the activity ratings are left out of the charts.

Skills are model-extracted

The LinkedIn set's skill lists come from the dataset author's own extraction step, so they reflect that model's vocabulary as much as the employer's. Required and preferred are not separated.

Mention isn't time

Posting emphasis counts what is written down. Most mentions are phase-neutral (tools, soft skills, credentials); the share that qualified is printed under each chart.

One snapshot, one series

The LinkedIn set is January 2024 only. The dated series covers one title (data analyst) from Google Jobs, with a thin August–November 2024.

Lexicon gaps

Git, Kubernetes, Jenkins, microservices, nursing and patient care have no lexicon entry, so they sit in the "not in the lexicon yet" panel. The variant "Linux" resolves to the entry "Linux systems administration", which inflates that skill for software developers.

Boilerplate leaks in

Benefits text reaches the skill lists: "Pet insurance" and "Disability insurance" show up as knowledge for nurses and electricians, and "401(k) plan" had to be ruled phase-neutral by hand. Near-duplicate postings were common too: text dedup removed 21% of postings that survived company-title-location dedup (26% for software developers).

Title ambiguity

"Data Analyst" is a listed title for eight O*NET occupations; a hand decision maps it to Business Intelligence Analysts (15-2051.01). Title matching assigned 35% of US postings; 19% had ambiguous titles ("manager", "store manager", "physician") and 45% matched nothing. The page shows posting charts only for occupations with 50 or more assigned postings.
